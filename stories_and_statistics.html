Scurvy and an early controlled trial

At sea, a comparison of treatments replaced competing stories with directly observed evidence.

HMS SALISBURY · 1747

James Lind compares six remedies

Scurvy weakened sailors on long voyages. Lind selected 12 sailors with similar symptoms, kept their basic conditions alike, and divided them into six pairs. Each pair received a different supplement.

The two sailors given oranges and lemons improved most quickly. Lind published the account in A Treatise of the Scurvy in 1753.

ROTHAMSTED · 1920s

Could Muriel Bristol really tell?

At Rothamsted Experimental Station, phycologist Muriel Bristol said she could distinguish tea according to whether milk or tea entered the cup first.

Ronald A. Fisher framed a fair test: eight cups, four prepared each way, presented in randomized order. Bristol classified all eight correctly. Fisher later used the experiment in his 1935 book The Design of Experiments.

Clear claimTell milk-first from tea-first.
Fixed designFour cups of each type.
Random orderPrevent ordering clues.
Exact testCompare the result with chance.

Experiments create data. Statistics give the data shape.

No single summary is always best. The arithmetic mean, geometric mean, mode, and median answer different questions.

03

Four ways to describe a “typical” value

Watch each measure work, then choose the one that matches the structure of your data.

Arithmetic mean

Pool the total, then share it equally.

(2 + 4 + 6 + 8) ÷ 4 = 5

Geometric mean

The equal multiplicative factor. Useful for ratios, growth rates, and proportional change.

⁴√(1 × 2 × 8 × 16) = 4

Mode

The value that appears most often. It also works for categories.

2, 3, 3, 3, 5, 5 → mode = 3

Median

Sort the values and select the middle. Resistant to extreme values.

2, 3, 7, 12, 50 → median = 7

Choose by meaning—four practical examples

Arithmetic mean · Exam scoresScores of 60, 70, and 80 have a mean of 70. Use it when values combine additively and every value should influence the result.
Geometric mean · Investment growthAn investment grows by 10% one year and 20% the next. Use the geometric mean for the typical compounded growth rate.
Mode · Shoe inventoryIf size 42 is sold more often than any other size, 42 is the mode. It tells a shop which single size is most common.
Median · House pricesOne mansion can pull the arithmetic mean upward. The median gives the middle sale price and better represents a typical home.
04

Distribution, spread, and uncertainty

A center is only part of the story. These concepts show the shape of the data, how observations vary, and how precisely a sample estimates a mean.

Statistical distribution

A distribution shows which values occur and how often. Look for its center, spread, shape, gaps, and unusual observations.

Sample variance

Variance averages the squared distances from the sample mean. Squaring prevents positive and negative deviations from cancelling.

s² = Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1)

Standard deviation

Standard deviation is the square root of variance, returning spread to the original measurement units.

s = √6.67 ≈ 2.58

Standard error of the mean

The standard error estimates how much a sample mean would vary across repeated samples. Larger samples give more precise means.

SE = s ÷ √n
Do not confuse them: SD describes variation among individual observations; SE describes uncertainty in the estimated mean.

Monte Carlo methods for uncertainty

When uncertain inputs affect a model, simulation shows the range and probability of possible outcomes—not just one best guess.

MONTE CARLO SIMULATION
“What could happen under uncertainty?”

The model and decision rules stay fixed; uncertain inputs change from run to run according to chosen probability distributions.

  • Estimate the full distribution of an output.
  • Measure percentiles, ranges, and expected values.
  • Estimate the probability of crossing a risk threshold.
01
Define variablesIdentify uncertain inputs, outputs, constraints, and plausible ranges.
02
Assign distributionsChoose defensible probability models and account for correlations.
03
Set parametersCalibrate distributions, fix assumptions, and define risk thresholds.
04
Run simulationsDraw new inputs and evaluate the same model thousands of times.
05
Analyze resultsSummarize variability, percentiles, sensitivity, and decision risk.
Example: annual project costDemand, material price, and downtime are uncertain. One run produces one possible annual cost; 10,000 runs produce a distribution of possible costs.
Decision questionInstead of asking only “What is the average cost?”, ask “What is the probability that cost exceeds the €130 million budget?”
Important: Monte Carlo simulation propagates the assumptions you provide. Poor distributions, omitted dependencies, or a weak model can produce precise-looking but misleading results.
06

One Factor at a Time

OFAT changes one input while every other input is held fixed. It is easy to understand, but it cannot reveal how factors work together.

TWO-FACTOR EXAMPLE

Optimizing an oven process

Suppose product quality depends on temperature and baking time. Start at 180°C for 30 minutes, where the quality score is 70.

Choose a baseline. Set temperature to 180°C and time to 30 minutes.
Vary temperature only. Test 160°C, 180°C, and 200°C while time remains 30 minutes.
Hold the selected temperature. At 200°C, test 20, 30, and 40 minutes.
Select the best observed setting. OFAT chooses 200°C and 30 minutes, with score 78.
What OFAT misses: the combination 180°C and 40 minutes scores 92. The effect of time depends on temperature—this is an interaction.

When it can be useful

Simple troubleshooting, adjusting one controllable setting, or local refinement when interactions are already known to be negligible.

Main limitations

Effects depend on the chosen baseline and search order. OFAT cannot estimate interactions, can miss the best combination, and repeated multi-level searches may become inefficient.

Why factorial designs are different: they vary factors together, so main effects and interactions can be estimated within one planned experiment.
07

Factorial Design and Design of Experiments

Design of Experiments changes several controllable factors together according to a planned structure, making cause-and-effect questions testable.

DESIGN OF EXPERIMENTS
“What changes when I deliberately move the factors?”

A factorial design tests combinations of factor levels. This reveals which factors matter and whether the effect of one factor depends on another.

With two factors at two levels, a full factorial has 2² = 4 combinations. Three two-level factors require 2³ = 8 combinations.

FactorsInputs deliberately changed by the experimenter—for example, temperature and baking time.
LevelsThe settings tested for each factor—for example, 180°C and 200°C.
ResponseThe measured outcome used to judge the process—for example, product-quality score.
EffectsChanges in the response caused by factors alone or by factors acting together.

Treatment combinations

A treatment combination is the complete set of factor levels assigned to one experimental unit. In the oven’s 2² design, 180°C + 30 minutes is one of four treatment combinations.

Fixed and random effects

A fixed effect compares deliberately chosen levels, so conclusions concern those settings. A random effect represents variation among levels viewed as a sample from a wider population, such as batches, operators, or sites. A model may contain both.

Confounding

Effects are confounded when the design does not contain enough independent comparisons to separate them. If A and B always move together, their effects are indistinguishable. Fractional factorial designs use planned confounding, called aliasing.

From a question to a defensible conclusion

01 · DefineState the objective, response, factors, and practical effect size.
02 · DesignChoose levels, combinations, replication, blocks, and run order.
03 · RunHold the protocol consistent and record every independent observation.
04 · AnalyzeEstimate effects, interactions, uncertainty, and model adequacy.
05 · ConfirmTest the selected setting in a separate confirmation run.
RandomizationProtects comparisons from time trends and unknown ordering effects.
ReplicationProvides independent information about experimental variation and precision.
BlockingGroups similar experimental units so known nuisance variation can be separated.
DoE is broader than one design: full and fractional factorial designs support screening and interaction detection; response-surface designs support curvature and optimization. The appropriate design depends on the question, resources, and assumptions.

Factor Coding and the Design Matrix

Coding translates physical settings into model columns. The design matrix is the numerical record of which treatment combination was used in each run.

FROM EXPERIMENT TO MODEL
“How do factor settings become estimable effects?”

For the oven experiment, code 180°C and 30 minutes as −1, and 200°C and 40 minutes as +1. Every experimental run becomes one row.

The interaction column is computed as AB = A × B.

quality = β₀ + βA·A + βB·B + βAB·AB
Symmetric coding−1, +1Natural for two-level factorials. In a balanced full factorial, centered columns make effect calculations simple and orthogonal.
Reference coding0, 1One level becomes the reference. Coefficients describe changes relative to that baseline; this is common for categorical variables.
Three-level quantitative−1, 0, +1A centered linear code for low, middle, and high settings. Add a quadratic contrast to represent curvature.
Orthogonal contrastslinear, quadratic…Useful for ordered levels. Under suitable balance and spacing, separate contrasts estimate distinct response patterns.
Standardizationz = (x − x̄)/sRescales a continuous variable for numerical convenience. It is not a replacement for a well-planned experimental design.

How the matrix connects to the earlier concepts

Treatment combinationsEach row contains the complete factor settings assigned to one experimental run.
Main effects and interactionsFactor columns estimate main effects; product columns such as AB estimate interactions.
ConfoundingIf two model columns are identical or linearly dependent, their effects cannot be estimated separately.
Fixed and random effectsFixed effects use the usual model matrix X. Random effects add grouping and variance structure, commonly represented by a separate matrix Z.

Full and Fractional Factorial Designs

A full factorial runs every combination. A fractional factorial deliberately runs a structured subset, reducing cost while accepting specific confounding assumptions.

THREE TWO-LEVEL FACTORS
“Do we need all eight runs?”

With factors A, B, and C at two levels, the full design contains 2³ = 8 treatment combinations.

A half-fraction contains 2³⁻¹ = 4 carefully selected combinations. It saves four runs, but some effects become aliased.

Full factorial

  • Runs every factor-level combination.
  • Separates all factorial effects supported by the design.
  • Best when the factor count is manageable and interactions matter.
  • Run count grows quickly: 2⁵ = 32 and 2⁸ = 256.

Fractional factorial

  • Runs a balanced, deliberately selected fraction.
  • Useful for screening many factors with a limited run budget.
  • Relies on assumptions that higher-order interactions are negligible.
  • Creates a known alias structure that must guide interpretation.

Example: a one-half fraction with C = AB

The defining relation is I = ABC. Multiplying this relation by each effect gives the alias pairs:

A ≡ BC   ·   B ≡ AC   ·   C ≡ AB

If the estimated A contrast is large, this four-run design cannot determine whether A caused it, BC caused it, or both contributed. This is a Resolution III design.

Resolution describes what is aliased

Resolution IIIMain effects are aliased with two-factor interactions. Useful only when those interactions are plausibly small.
Resolution IVMain effects are clear of two-factor interactions, but two-factor interactions are aliased with each other.
Resolution VMain effects and two-factor interactions are mutually clear; two-factor interactions may be aliased with three-factor interactions.
Selection rule: use a full factorial when the run count is practical or interactions are central. Use a suitable-resolution fraction for screening when resources are limited, then follow important findings with augmentation or confirmation runs. Replication or other error information is still needed to quantify experimental noise.

Space-Filling Designs for Global Exploration

When factors are continuous and simulations are expensive, space-filling designs spread a limited number of runs across the entire input region.

CONTINUOUS DESIGN SPACE
“How can we learn the global response without evaluating a dense grid?”

A grid with four settings per factor needs 4² = 16 runs for two factors, but 4⁶ = 4,096 runs for six factors. The growth depends on both the number of factors and levels.

A space-filling design uses a chosen run budget to cover the domain, supporting surrogate models, global sensitivity analysis, and computer experiments.

Latin hypercube samplingDivides every input dimension into equal-probability strata and places one run in each stratum of every dimension.
Maximin LHSSelects or optimizes an LHS to increase the minimum distance between design points, improving multidimensional spread.
Orthogonal or optimized LHSAdds balance or low-correlation criteria. Correlated real inputs still require an appropriate dependence model.
Low-discrepancy sequencesSobol’, Halton, and related sequences progressively fill the unit cube for quasi-Monte Carlo integration and exploration.
Maximum-entropy designsPlace points to maximize information or entropy under a chosen covariance model, often for surrogate-model training.

Typical computer-experiment workflow

01 · DomainDefine valid ranges, constraints, and transformations.
02 · BudgetChoose the number of affordable simulation runs.
03 · DesignGenerate and diagnose the space-filling points.
04 · EvaluateRun the simulator at each selected point.
05 · EmulateFit and validate a surrogate such as a Gaussian process.
06 · UseExplore sensitivity, uncertainty, or optimization globally.
Factorial designUses structured level combinations to estimate effects and interactions clearly, usually over a small set of chosen levels.
Space-filling designCovers a continuous region to learn an unknown response surface. Classical factorial contrasts are not its primary objective.
Monte Carlo samplingDraws according to an input probability distribution to propagate uncertainty. Space filling instead prioritizes coverage of the design region.
Design carefully: there is no universal rule that a full factorial becomes infeasible after a fixed number of factors. Feasibility depends on levels, run cost, constraints, and available budget. High-dimensional space filling also becomes difficult, so diagnostics and sequential augmentation matter.

Response Surface Methodology

RSM models curvature in a response and uses that local model to understand, improve, or optimize a process.

LOCAL QUADRATIC MODEL
“Where should we move the factors next?”

After important quantitative factors have been identified, center and axial runs can augment a factorial design so curvature becomes estimable.

y = β₀ + Σβᵢxᵢ + Σβᵢᵢxᵢ² + Σβᵢⱼxᵢxⱼ + ε

The fitted surface is a local approximation. Its stationary point may be a maximum, minimum, or saddle point.

Central Composite Design

Combines a factorial or fractional-factorial core, replicated center points, and axial—or star—points. The axial distance α can be chosen for properties such as rotatability.

Box–Behnken Design

Uses combinations where two factors vary while others remain at their centers, plus center points. It avoids all-factor corner combinations and can require fewer runs in some dimensions.

Face-Centered CCD

Places axial points at ±1 on the faces of the factor cube. This respects the original bounds and is easy to run, but it is generally not rotatable.

A sequential RSM strategy

01 · ScreenIdentify a small set of important quantitative factors.
02 · CenterAdd replicated center runs to test for curvature and estimate error.
03 · AugmentAdd axial or Box–Behnken points that support quadratic estimation.
04 · FitEstimate linear, interaction, and squared terms.
05 · DiagnoseCheck residuals, lack of fit, hierarchy, and prediction uncertainty.
06 · ConfirmRun the proposed optimum independently before operational use.
Contour interpretationElliptical or curved contours reveal curvature, interaction, and the direction of improvement.
Multiple responsesDesirability functions or constrained optimization can balance quality, cost, and safety objectives.
Sequential learningIf the optimum lies outside the current region, move the region and fit a new local model rather than extrapolating far away.
Use locally: a quadratic model is an approximation over the studied region. Respect process constraints, preserve model hierarchy, quantify prediction uncertainty, and confirm the recommended setting experimentally.

One-Way ANOVA

Analysis of variance tests whether several population means are equal by comparing variation between treatment means with variation among observations inside treatments.

SINGLE-FACTOR EXPERIMENT
“Are the observed mean differences larger than ordinary experimental noise?”

Factor: hardwood concentration, with four treatment levels: 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%.

Response: tensile strength, with six independent replicates at each level.

Full-Factorial ANOVA Example and Replication Planning

A three-factor, two-level experiment has eight treatment combinations. Independent replication supplies pure error, supports F-tests, and controls the precision of estimated means and effects.

2³ FULL FACTORIAL
“Which factors and interactions change the response?”

Factors A, B, and C each have low and high levels. All 2³ = 8 combinations are tested twice, giving 16 independent runs.

The entire 16-run order should be randomized. Each pair of observations at a treatment combination estimates its cell mean and contributes to pure experimental error.

Geometric coding− − −, + − −, …, + + +Uses low and high signs directly for A, B, and C.
Letter labels(1), a, b, ab, c, ac, bc, abcA letter appears when that factor is at its high level.
Binary coding000, 100, 010, …, 111Uses 0 for low and 1 for high. The analysis here uses symmetric −1/+1 coding.

Estimated effects from differences in means

For any main factor or interaction X, split the observations by the sign of its coded column and subtract the two group means:

Effect(X) = ȳX=+1 − ȳX=−1 = Contrast(X) / [r × 2k−1]

Here, r = 2 replicates and k = 3 factors, so every contrast is divided by 2 × 2² = 8. For an interaction, multiply the relevant coded columns first—for example, AC = A × C. The effects below are ordered by absolute effect size.

1 · MAIN FACTORFactor C+306.125929.125 − 623.000 = +306.125F = 166.41, p ≈ 1.23×10⁻⁶. This is the largest effect.

3 · MAIN FACTORFactor A−101.625725.250 − 826.875 = −101.625F = 18.34, p ≈ 0.00268. Interpret A together with AC.

Reading the sign: a positive effect means the mean response is higher in the +1 group; a negative effect means it is lower. Effect size ranks practical magnitude, while the F-test uses MSPE = 2,252.56 to judge that magnitude against experimental noise.
Hierarchy and interpretation: when an interaction is important, retain and interpret its component main effects. Average main effects alone can hide conditional behavior.

Pure error from replication

Compute each cell mean. Compare every observation with the mean of its treatment combination.
Pool within-cell deviations. SSPE = 18,020.5 across the eight combinations.
Count pure-error df. With r = 2, dfPE = 8(r−1) = 8.
Estimate variance. MSPE = SSPE/dfPE = 2,252.56, so the estimated run-level SD is 47.46.

Why the F Distribution

The ANOVA table compares two variance estimates. The distribution changes with the column: from the observations, to the sums of squares, to the mean squares, and then to their ratio.

ALONG THE ANOVA TABLE
“Why does the table end with F, rather than a normal or a chi-square probability?”

Follow the variables that appear in the table. Each one has the distribution that justifies the next column. The unknown σ² stays in the arithmetic until two variance estimates are divided.

1 · Observations yNormal, and the table continuesUnder the model the observations are independent and normal, each with a treatment mean and one common variance σ². A probability from that normal curve would require the value of σ². The experiment does not know it, so the ANOVA table does not stop at the raw data.
2 · DeviationsThe pieces that will be squaredSplit each observation into the grand mean, a treatment deviation, and a residual. The three pieces add back to y. These are the pieces the next column squares.
3 · Sums of squaresChi-square after dividing by σ²SST = SSfactor + SSerror. A sum of squares of normal deviations, divided by the unknown σ², is chi-square. SSerror / σ² is chi-square on the error degrees of freedom whether or not the factor matters. SSfactor / σ² is central chi-square only when the null hypothesis is true and the factor adds no extra variation. One chi-square still contains σ², so a sum-of-squares column alone is not a probability.
4 · Degrees of freedomThe count that makes a varianceFactor df is the number of independent treatment comparisons. Error df is the number of leftover independent residuals. Dividing a sum of squares by its degrees of freedom turns that sum into a variance estimate.
5 · Mean squaresChi-square divided by its dfMS = SS / df. MSerror estimates σ². Under the null, MSfactor also estimates σ². MS / σ² is that chi-square divided by its degrees of freedom. σ² is still unknown, so a mean square is not yet a tail probability.
6 · The F ratioTwo variances, and σ² cancelsF = MSfactor / MSerror. The same σ² is inside both mean squares, so it cancels. The ratio of two independent chi-squares, each divided by its df, is the F distribution with those two degrees of freedom. The ANOVA table ends with F, and the p-value is the upper tail of that F curve, not a normal or a chi-square curve. A large F means the factor mean square is large relative to the residual variance.

From one column to the next

Each formula is the distribution that appears when that column is formed.

y = grand mean + treatment deviation + residual
residuals independent and normal, mean 0, variance σ²
SST = SSfactor + SSerror
SSerror / σ² ~ χ² on the error df
SSfactor / σ² ~ central χ² on the factor df, only if H₀ is true
MS = SS / df
MS / σ² = (SS / σ²) / df
F = MSfactor / MSerror ~ F(factor df, error df) under H₀

The one-degree-of-freedom bridge. When the numerator has 1 df, the F reference and the t reference are the same comparison written two ways. t is that bridge, not a separate destination. ANOVA needs F because the numerator can have more than one degree of freedom.

F(1, ν) = tν²

F is used because the ANOVA table compares two variance estimates. The distribution changes exactly when the variable changes: from the observations, to the sums of squares, to the mean squares, to their ratio.
The central F is the null reference. It needs independent normal errors and independent sums of squares. Both mean squares estimate σ² only when the factor adds no extra variation. If the factor is present, the numerator chi-square is non-central and the ratio tends to be larger. Non-normal errors or unequal variances change the meaning of the tail probability.
